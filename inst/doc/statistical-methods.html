Complex survey estimation

A survey respondent stands for many people, and not the same number each: the final weight says how many. The weights fix the estimate; the design (how the sample was drawn) fixes its uncertainty, and ignoring it usually understates the interval. svydes() records the design, svyest() gives means, totals, proportions and ratios with design-based standard errors, and svycmp() tests differences between groups.

Two ways to the variance

Public-use files from Statistics Canada and Health Canada ship replicate weights: hundreds of alternative weight columns, each a resampled version of the design. The estimate is recomputed under each and the spread of those estimates is the variance. Other surveys ship the strata and primary sampling units (PSUs), and the variance comes from Taylor linearization.

set.seed(1)
n <- 600
d <- data.frame(region = sample(c("north", "south", "east"), n, replace = TRUE),
                age = sample(c("15-24", "25-49", "50+"), n, replace = TRUE),
                used = rbinom(n, 1, 0.3), drinks = rpois(n, 3), w = runif(n, 40, 160))
# a stratified design: variance by linearization
lin <- svydes(d, w = "w", strata = "region")
lin
#> Survey design: 600 respondents, weights summing to 60,110
#> Variance: Taylor linearization, 3 strata, 600 PSUs
# a replicate design: 200 bootstrap weights, made here by resampling within region
reps <- sapply(1:200, function(r) {
  m <- numeric(n)
  for (g in unique(d$region)) {
    i <- which(d$region == g)
    m[i] <- tabulate(sample(length(i), replace = TRUE), length(i)) * length(i) / (length(i) - 1)
  }
  d$w * m
})
repdes <- svydes(d, w = "w", reps = reps, type = "bootstrap")
repdes
#> Survey design: 600 respondents, weights summing to 60,110
#> Variance: 200 replicate weights, bootstrap; scale 0.005, centred on the full-sample estimate

Estimates by domain

A domain (a region, an age group) is estimated inside the whole design: a respondent outside it counts zero, but stays in the design. That is what makes the subgroup’s variance right.

e <- svyest(repdes, d, "used", by = "age", stat = "proportion", lvls = "1")
e
#> Survey proportion of used by age (bootstrap variance, 95% wilson interval)
#>    age level estimate      se  lower  upper     cv   n flag
#>  15-24     1   0.3681 0.03560 0.3018 0.4398  9.671 206   NA
#>  25-49     1   0.2967 0.03063 0.2375 0.3635 10.324 199   NA
#>    50+     1   0.2333 0.03067 0.1790 0.2982 13.147 195   NA
plot(e)

svyest(lin, d, "drinks", by = "region")
#> Survey mean of drinks by region (linearization variance, 95% wald interval)
#>  region estimate     se lower upper    cv   n flag
#>    east    2.857 0.1245 2.614 3.101 4.356 186   NA
#>   north    2.822 0.1254 2.577 3.068 4.442 220   NA
#>   south    3.046 0.1282 2.794 3.297 4.210 194   NA

Proportions get the modified Wilson interval of SAS PROC SURVEYFREQ (the effective sample size \(n/\mathrm{Deff}\), adjusted for the design’s degrees of freedom and never above \(n\)); it stays inside 0 to 100% and behaves near the ends. Domains with fewer than 30 respondents are flagged, the rule Health Canada uses to suppress estimates in its data tool; here nothing is withheld, and the flag travels with the row.

Comparing groups

Two overlapping intervals do not mean two groups are the same, and two separate intervals leave out the covariance between the estimates. svycmp() takes the difference within the design:

cmp <- svycmp(repdes, d, "used", by = "region", stat = "proportion", lvls = "1",
                      ref = "north")
cmp
#> Survey proportion of used against north (95% interval)
#>  domain level estimate reference difference      se    lower   upper p_value
#>    east     1   0.3074    0.3082  -0.000804 0.04468 -0.08838 0.08677  0.9856
#>   south     1   0.2862    0.3082  -0.021992 0.05139 -0.12272 0.07874  0.6687
plot(cmp)

When you only have the published table

Often there is no microdata, only the published table: an estimate and its 95% limits for each group. The limits carry the design. A standard error is the limits’ width over \(2z\), and it includes the design effect of the survey’s weights and clusters. scests() puts each Statistics Canada estimate beside its limits. indgap(), dstntst() and ordcmp() then compare groups from them.

They treat the groups as independent samples. Disjoint domains of one survey nearly are, but not exactly, since they share strata and clusters. The exact comparison needs the bootstrap weights, which Statistics Canada holds in its Research Data Centres.

Perceived mental health of First Nations, Metis and Inuit adults living off reserve, 2019/2022 (table 13-10-0924-01):

h <- indiex("health")
mh <- h[h$period == "2019/2022" & h$age == "Total, 18 years and over" &
          h$indicator == "Perceived mental health, very good or excellent", ]
indgap(mh, "group", "estimate", "lower", "upper", qual = "quality")[
  , c("group", "difference", "diflo", "difup")]
#>           group difference     diflo      difup
#> 1 First Nations      -15.7 -18.53593 -12.864070
#> 2         Métis       -8.7 -11.58531  -5.814692
#> 3         Inuit      -13.7 -20.16568  -7.234321
dstntst(mh[mh$group != "Non-Indigenous", ], "group", "estimate", "lower", "upper")
#> Distinctions test across 3 groups: Q = 11.97 on 2 df, p = 0.00252, I^2 = 83%
#> Pooled (inverse-variance) 49.11; the groups differ: report each one, not the pooled figure

Linearization or replicates, the same answer

On the same data the two routes agree closely when the replicates come from the same design:

rbind(linearization = svyest(lin, d, "drinks")[, c("estimate", "se")],
      bootstrap = svyest(repdes, d, "drinks")[, c("estimate", "se")])
#>  estimate      se
#>     2.906 0.07295
#>     2.906 0.07324

Every variance formula here is checked in the package tests against the survey package to machine precision: linearized means, totals, ratios, proportions and domains with a finite population correction; JK1, bootstrap and Fay replicate designs; and differences between domains.

Causal effects with cross-fitted double machine learning

plrcf() estimates the average effect of a treatment on an outcome when the treated and the untreated differ in measured ways (confounders). It fits the partially linear model

\[ Y = \theta D + g(X) + \varepsilon, \qquad D = m(X) + v, \]

by the double machine learning recipe of Chernozhukov et al. (2018): the outcome and the treatment are each predicted from the confounders \(X\) on folds the row was not part of (cross-fitting), and \(\theta\) is the regression of the outcome residuals on the treatment residuals (DML2), with a sandwich standard error. When the effect is the same for everyone, \(\theta\) is the average treatment effect (ATE); otherwise it is a variance-weighted average of the individual effects. exprspplr() bootstraps the same estimator for a continuous exposure (see the air-pollution vignette). The nuisances \(g\) and \(m\) are ordinary least squares fits, so \(\theta\) is consistent when they are linear in the columns given; add squares, interactions or splines as columns where they are not.

A known effect, recovered

Confounding biases the simple comparison; the cross-fitted estimate removes it. Here the true effect of d on y is 2, and x1 drives both:

set.seed(42)
n <- 2000
sim <- data.frame(x1 = rnorm(n), x2 = rnorm(n))
sim$d <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(1.5 * sim$x1))
sim$y <- 2 * sim$d + 3 * sim$x1 + sim$x2 + rnorm(n)
mean(sim$y[sim$d == 1]) - mean(sim$y[sim$d == 0])   # biased by x1
#> [1] 5.373073
fit <- plrcf(sim, "y", "d", c("x1", "x2"))
fit$ate
#> [1] 2.039439
fit$ate + c(-1.96, 1.96) * fit$se
#> [1] 1.938179 2.140699

The NSW job-training experiment, and what observational data can recover

LaLonde (1986) took the National Supported Work Demonstration, a randomised job-training programme of the 1970s, and asked whether the econometric methods of the day could recover its experimental effect on 1978 earnings when the randomised controls are replaced by a survey sample (the PSID). Dehejia and Wahba (1999) kept the men with 1974 earnings: 185 treated, 260 randomised controls, and 2,490 PSID-1 comparison men. The three files are on data.rmorie.com (rmbl login once), checked against the SHA-256 the manifest lists:

tr <- dload("nsw_dw/treated")
ct <- dload("nsw_dw/control")
ps <- dload("nsw_dw/psid_controls")

Or, without signing in, from Rajeev Dehejia’s own pages (whitespace-separated text):

cols <- c("treat", "age", "educ", "black", "hisp", "married", "nodegree", "re74", "re75", "re78")
nber <- function(f) {
  dest <- file.path(tempdir(), f)
  bldl(paste0("https://users.nber.org/~rdehejia/data/", f), dest)
  stats::setNames(utils::read.table(dest), cols)
}
tr <- nber("nswre74_treated.txt")
ct <- nber("nswre74_control.txt")
ps <- nber("psid_controls.txt")

Adjusting for the eight pre-programme covariates turns a fifteen-thousand-dollar penalty into a small positive effect whose interval covers the experimental one, though it sits well below it and is far less precise. Two things limit it. The nuisances are linear, so the large earnings gap is adjusted for only as a straight line; and the overlap is thin: most PSID men earned more before the programme than almost any treated man, so the comparison rests on the few who look like them. Dehejia and Wahba show that trimming the comparison group to the region of common support (on an estimated propensity score) brings the observational estimate close to the experiment; with plrcf(), restrict obs to that region before fitting. The cross-fit split moves the estimate by about a sixth of a standard error, which is why the seed and the number of folds are arguments.

Overlap and balance

Two checks belong with every weighting estimate: whether the two groups overlap (each person has a real chance of either treatment) and whether weighting balances the covariates. On simulated data, where the true effect is 3:

set.seed(7)
n <- 1500
d <- data.frame(age = runif(n, 18, 70), income = rnorm(n))
d$t <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-2 + 0.04 * d$age + 0.5 * d$income))
d$y <- 10 + 3 * d$t + 0.2 * d$age + d$income + rnorm(n)
fit <- aipwcf(d, "y", "t", c("age", "income"), lrn = "ols")
fit
#> Cross-fitted AIPW, ATE: 1500 rows, 684 treated
#>  estimate      se lower upper p_value
#>     3.071 0.05606 2.961 3.181       0
#> Propensities from 0.086 to 0.903; 0 clipped
plot(fit)

plot(fit, type = "estimate")

pr <- attr(fit, "propensity")
e <- pmin(pmax(pr$propensity, 0.01), 0.99)
d$ipw <- ifelse(d$t == 1, 1 / e, 1 / (1 - e))
plot(baltab(d, "t", c("age", "income")),
     after = baltab(d, "t", c("age", "income"), w = "ipw"))

The groups overlap where both curves have mass; after weighting, both covariates fall below the 0.1 line.

Evaluating a policy from administrative panels

A reform reaches some places before others: a new criminal procedure rolled out district by district, a policing program adopted city by city, a ward boundary redrawn. Agencies publish the outcomes as counts or rates by place and period. This vignette covers the designs that turn such a table into an estimate of the reform’s effect, with honest uncertainty. Each estimator gives the same answer as the reference implementation (fixest, did, bacondecomp) to rounding error, and none of them needs a package outside base R.

The examples use polex(): twenty districts in four regions over 36 months, with a reform that reaches the regions in three stages (months 13, 19 and 25) and never reaches the fourth. The numbers are made up, with a known effect: the reform lowers the crime rate by 3.

Staggered adoption

When districts adopt at different times, the two-way estimate uses districts already treated as controls for later ones. bacondcmp() shows how much weight those comparisons get:

b <- bacondcmp(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "reformed")
tapply(b$weight, b$type, sum)
#> earlier vs later later vs earlier treated vs never 
#>        0.2093023        0.2093023        0.5813953
plot(b)

stgatt() avoids them: each cohort is compared only with districts not yet treated (Callaway and Sant’Anna), or the regression has a coefficient for every cohort and period (Wooldridge). The bootstrap resamples whole districts:

s <- stgatt(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "refmonth", reps = 99, seed = 1)
s$overall
#>    estimate        se     lower     upper
#> 1 -2.825844 0.3400471 -3.492324 -2.159364
plot(s)

For a treatment that can switch off as well as on, swtdid() compares the units that switch with those that do not, in the period they switch, with a placebo one period earlier:

Inference from the assignment

With four regions there are only 24 ways to assign the three stages and the never-treated region. perminf() tries every one, and gives the share at least as extreme as the real assignment. With two outcomes it also gives Westfall and Young’s stepdown p-values, which hold the chance of any false positive at the nominal level:

r <- perminf(p, "district", "month", c("crmrate", "arrrate"), "refmonth",
                           cl = "region")

#>   outcome   estimate  statistic    p_value     pstpdn assignments exact
#> 1 crmrate -3.2316395 -19.516735 0.04166667 0.04166667          24  TRUE
#> 2 arrrate -0.2136969  -3.638159 0.12500000 0.12500000          24  TRUE
plot(r, type = "histogram")

loounit() checks that no single district drives the estimate:

plot(loounit(p, "district", "month", "crmrate", "reformed"))

Measures for justice data

Three measures prepare the outcomes these designs take:

  • sevidx() combines offence rates, weighted by each offence’s severity (for example its mean statutory sentence);
  • piperate() gives the rate at which one stage of the justice process follows another, with the input’s window set before the outcome’s, because cases take time;

Falsification controls and power curves: is the statistic real?

A capsule can prove that a statistic was computed from exactly these bytes. It cannot, by itself, say whether the statistic means anything. Two complementary checks address that: a falsification control asks whether the statistic survives deliberate attempts to break it, and a power curve asks whether the procedure could have detected an effect of a given size at all. Both are borrowed from the refutation tests of causal-inference practice (placebo treatments, random subsets, permutation) and made to run on any statistic you can write as a function of the data.

Falsification controls

cpsfals() takes the data, a statistic (a function of the data frame) and the name of the treatment column, and runs the controls that a real association must pass:

  • permutation: shuffle the treatment; the statistic should collapse toward its null;
  • placebo: replace the treatment with noise; the statistic should collapse;
  • subsets: recompute on random subsets; the statistic should stay stable.
set.seed(1)
d <- data.frame(x = rnorm(200))
d$y <- 0.8 * d$x + rnorm(200)

real <- cpsfals(d, function(z) cor(z$x, z$y), trt = "x", n = 199, seed = 42)
real$controls[, c("control", "passed")]
#>               control passed
#> 1         permutation   TRUE
#> 2             placebo   TRUE
#> 3 random_common_cause   TRUE
#> 4    subset_stability   TRUE

A statistic that ignores the data fails the controls that can see it. A constant “0.5” passes the subset check (it is perfectly stable) and fails the permutation check (it does not collapse when the treatment is destroyed):

fake <- cpsfals(d, function(z) 0.5, trt = "x", n = 199, seed = 42)
fake$controls[fake$controls$control == "permutation", "passed"]
#> [1] FALSE

This is the point of running controls rather than looking at a p-value: the p-value of a constant is undefined, but its behaviour under permutation is not.

Power curves

A null result is only informative if the procedure could have found something. cpspwr() injects an effect of known size into the data, re-runs the statistic with its permutation test, and reports the detection rate at each size:

set.seed(1)
d <- data.frame(x = rnorm(120), y = rnorm(120))
inj <- function(z, size) {
  z$y <- z$y + size * z$x
  z
}

pw <- cpspwr(d, function(z) cor(z$x, z$y), inj,
                    sizes = c(0, 0.3, 0.6), trt = "x",
                    n = 99, reps = 5, seed = 42)
pw$curve
#>   size detected reps rate
#> 1  0.0        0    5    0
#> 2  0.3        5    5    1
#> 3  0.6        5    5    1

The rate at size 0 estimates the false-positive rate. With few repetitions it is usually 0, because alpha is small (five draws at 0.05 come up empty about three times in four), so read it as a sanity check that it is not large, not as an estimate of alpha:

pw$curve[pw$curve$size == 0, "rate"]
#> [1] 0

Use more reps for a real study; the example keeps them small so the vignette builds quickly. The curve answers the question a reviewer will ask of a null finding: “what effect size would you have detected with this data?”

Families of statistics: the multiple-testing correction

When several statistics are examined, the smallest p-value among them is not the finding. falsfam() applies a multiple-testing correction across a family of falsification results, so one real association is still reported as real and the noise is not:

set.seed(1)
d <- data.frame(x = rnorm(150))
d$y <- 0.6 * d$x + rnorm(150)
d$a <- rnorm(150)
d$b <- rnorm(150)
fam <- list(
  real    = cpsfals(d, function(z) cor(z$x, z$y), trt = "x", n = 199, seed = 1),
  noisea = cpsfals(d, function(z) cor(z$a, z$y), trt = "a", n = 199, seed = 2),
  noiseb = cpsfals(d, function(z) cor(z$b, z$y), trt = "b", n = 199, seed = 3)
)
falsfam(fam)
#> ── Family of 3, corrected by holm at alpha 0.05 ──────────────────
#>   real                   p 0.005      adjusted 0.015      survives
#>   noisea                 p 0.955      adjusted 1          
#>   noiseb                 p 0.995      adjusted 1          
#> ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
#>   at the permutation floor (more permutations would be needed to say more): real
#> ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

It also accepts bare p-values, which is what stops the smallest of several from being read as the finding:

falsfam(c(a = 0.01, b = 0.04, c = 0.2, d = 0.5))$results
#>   name    p adjusted survives
#> 1    a 0.01     0.04     TRUE
#> 2    b 0.04     0.12    FALSE
#> 3    c 0.20     0.40    FALSE
#> 4    d 0.50     0.50    FALSE

Sensitivity to an unmeasured confounder

For an observed risk ratio, the E-value (VanderWeele and Ding 2017) is the minimum strength of association an unmeasured confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to explain the result away:

evlrr(2)                       # needs a confounder associated by 3.41 with both
#> evlpoint 
#> 3.414214
evlrr(0.5)                     # a protective effect is inverted; same answer
#> evlpoint 
#> 3.414214
evlrr(2, lo = 0.9, hi = 4.4)   # an interval that includes the null needs nothing
#> evlpoint evllimit 
#> 3.414214 1.000000
evlrr(3, lo = 2.1, hi = 4.3)   # a strong result with a limit well above the null
#> evlpoint evllimit 
#> 5.449490 3.619868

Where this fits in a capsule

cpsrpt() runs the falsification controls alongside the schema, rule and drift checks when a statistic is supplied, so the capsule records not only that the number was computed from these bytes but that it survived its controls. A pre-registered hypothesis (prrgdecl(), prrgchk()) pins what was going to be tested before the data were seen, which is the other half of keeping a finding honest.

Small-area rates: expected counts, shrinkage, funnels and spatial autocorrelation

Published tables often give a count per region, division or facility. Comparing those counts fairly needs three steps: adjust for the population each unit serves (and its composition), stabilise the ratios of small units, and judge each unit against limits that widen as its expected count shrinks. This vignette walks the four tools that do that.

Expected counts by indirect standardisation

If the overall rate applied everywhere, how many events would each unit have? That is the expected count. Adjusting for size alone:

d <- data.frame(
  region = rep(c("North", "South", "East"), each = 2),
  age = rep(c("18 to 24", "25 to 49"), 3),
  n = c(30, 45, 12, 60, 8, 20),
  pop = c(1000, 6000, 900, 9000, 400, 3500)
)
expcnts(d$n, d$pop, d$region)
#>    area observed population expected
#> 1  East       28       3900 32.81250
#> 2 North       75       7000 58.89423
#> 3 South       72       9900 83.29327

Adjusting for size and age composition is indirect standardisation: each stratum’s overall rate is applied to the unit’s population in that stratum, then summed. This is the comparison that says something about the unit rather than about who lives there:

expcnts(d$n, d$pop, d$region, strata = d$age)
#>    area observed population expected
#> 1  East       28       3900 32.34430
#> 2 North       75       7000 62.27967
#> 3 South       72       9900 80.37603

Direct standardisation, for populations of different ages

Indirect standardisation asks what each unit would expect at the overall rates. Direct standardisation asks what rate each population would have with one standard age structure. It is the comparison to use when the populations’ ages differ a great deal. First Nations, Metis and Inuit populations, for example, are younger than the non-Indigenous population. agestd() gives the rates with the Fay-Feuer gamma interval, which holds for small counts. rategap() compares two of them. The counts below are made up, to show the computation:

r <- agestd(events = c(4, 9, 11, 6, 3, 40, 70, 90, 80, 60),
                     pop = c(9000, 8000, 6000, 4000, 2000, 300000, 320000, 350000, 340000, 300000),
                     std = c(30, 25, 20, 15, 10),
                     grp = rep(c("Inuit", "Non-Indigenous"), each = 5))
r
#>            group events      rate    lower    upper
#> 1          Inuit     33 115.62500 79.23967 165.0186
#> 2 Non-Indigenous    340  20.14102 17.93306  22.5839
rategap(r, "Inuit", "Non-Indigenous")
#>   group      reference    ratio  ratiolo ratioup difference    diflo    difup
#> 1 Inuit Non-Indigenous 5.740772 3.993798 8.25191   95.48398 55.54321 135.4247

For survey percentages rather than counts, stdprev() does the same.

Standardised ratios with exact intervals

The ratio of observed to expected is the standardised incidence ratio (SIR). stdinc() gives the exact Poisson interval, which is what makes a small count honest:

stdinc(obs = c(30, 12, 3), expd = c(20, 14, 5),
    area = c("North", "South", "East"))
#>    area observed expected       sir     lower    upper excess deficit
#> 1 North       30       20 1.5000000 1.0120437 2.141343   TRUE   FALSE
#> 2 South       12       14 0.8571429 0.4428982 1.497256  FALSE   FALSE
#> 3  East        3        5 0.6000000 0.1237344 1.753455  FALSE   FALSE
#>   significant
#> 1        TRUE
#> 2       FALSE
#> 3       FALSE

An observed count of three carries almost no information, and the interval says so rather than hiding it:

stdinc(3, 5)
#>   observed expected sir     lower    upper excess deficit significant
#> 1        3        5 0.6 0.1237344 1.753455  FALSE   FALSE       FALSE

Empirical Bayes shrinkage

A tiny unit’s raw ratio is extreme by chance. Empirical Bayes shrinks each ratio toward the overall experience in proportion to how little its own data say: the pooled ratios share a Gamma prior whose parameters are estimated from all units, and each unit’s posterior mean is a weighted average of its own ratio and the prior mean, weighted by its expected count (Clayton and Kaldor 1987).

ebrates(obs = c(30, 45, 2), expd = c(25, 50, 0.5),
         area = c("North", "South", "Tiny"))
#>    area observed expected sir        eb shrinkage       nu    alpha
#> 1 North       30     25.0 1.2 1.1073869 0.5141392 26.98066 26.45506
#> 2 South       45     50.0 0.9 0.9414767 0.3460211 26.98066 26.45506
#> 3  Tiny        2      0.5 4.0 1.0751472 0.9814506 26.98066 26.45506

The tiny area’s raw ratio of 4 is pulled sharply toward 1; the large areas barely move.

Funnel plots

A funnel plot draws control limits that narrow as the expected count grows (Spiegelhalter 2005): a ratio of 2 means nothing at an expected count of 2 and a great deal at 200. Units outside the limits are the ones worth a second look; the rest are consistent with the common rate.

fnllim(c(2, 20, 200))
#>   expected level lower upper
#> 1        2 0.950 0.000 2.500
#> 2       20 0.950 0.600 1.450
#> 3      200 0.950 0.865 1.140
#> 4        2 0.998 0.000 4.000
#> 5       20 0.998 0.400 1.750
#> 6      200 0.998 0.790 1.225
e <- seq(2, 200, length.out = 60)

     ylab = "observed / expected", main = "Funnel limits")

The levels argument sets which limits are drawn; 95% and 99.8% (about two and three standard deviations) are the conventional pair.

Spatial autocorrelation

Rates of neighbouring areas are rarely independent. Moran’s I measures whether similar values cluster in space, against a permutation null; neighbours is a list giving each area’s adjacent areas:

nb <- list(2L, c(1L, 3L), c(2L, 4L), c(3L, 5L), c(4L, 6L), 5L)   # six areas in a line

set.seed(1)
morani(c(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6), nb, nperm = 999L)[c("I", "p_value")]   # a gradient
#> $I
#> [1] 0.7142857
#> 
#> $p_value
#> [1] 0.011

set.seed(1)
morani(c(1, 6, 1, 6, 1, 6), nb, nperm = 999L)[c("I", "p_value")]   # alternating
#> $I
#> [1] -1
#> 
#> $p_value
#> [1] 0.199

The null expectation of I is not zero but \(-1/(n-1)\):

-1 / (6 - 1)
#> [1] -0.2

Positive autocorrelation in the residual ratios means neighbouring units share something the standardisation did not capture, and a funnel that treats them as independent is optimistic.

From a published table to a judgement

  1. expcnts() with the strata the table publishes.
  2. stdinc() for the ratios and their exact intervals.
  3. ebrates() where small units dominate.
  4. fnllim() to see which units are outside the common experience.
  5. morani() on the residuals before trusting independence.

Hawkes processes: self-exciting event series

Many administrative event series cluster: one incident raises the chance of another soon after. A Hawkes process (Hawkes 1971) models this directly. Its conditional intensity, the instantaneous event rate given the past, is

\[\lambda(t) = \mu + \sum_{t_i < t} \phi(t - t_i),\]

a baseline rate \(\mu\) plus a kernel \(\phi\) that each past event adds and that decays with the time since it. The integral of the kernel, \(n = \int_0^\infty \phi(s)\,ds\), is the branching ratio: the expected number of events each event directly triggers. When \(n < 1\) the process is stationary; \(n\) is the fraction of events that are “children” of earlier events rather than arrivals from the baseline, which is often the quantity of substantive interest.

Simulating a Hawkes process

Ogata’s thinning algorithm simulates the process exactly: propose events from an upper bound on the intensity and keep each with probability \(\lambda(t)/\lambda_{\max}\). Here is an exponential kernel \(\phi(s) = \alpha \beta e^{-\beta s}\), whose branching ratio is \(\alpha\):

simhwk <- function(mu, alpha, beta, horizon, seed = 1) {
  set.seed(seed)
  t <- 0
  events <- numeric(0)
  while (t < horizon) {
    lambdamax <- mu + alpha * beta * sum(exp(-beta * (t - events)))
    t <- t + stats::rexp(1, lambdamax)
    if (t >= horizon) break
    lambdat <- mu + alpha * beta * sum(exp(-beta * (t - events)))
    if (stats::runif(1) <= lambdat / lambdamax) events <- c(events, t)
  }
  events
}
times <- simhwk(mu = 1, alpha = 0.5, beta = 2, horizon = 300)
length(times)
#> [1] 559

Fitting

chwkfit() maximises the exact log-likelihood with an analytic gradient in C (projected BFGS, with the parameters bounded). theta holds the baseline, the branching ratio and the kernel parameter:

fit <- chwkfit(times, hor = 300, kernel = "exponential")
fit$theta
#> [1] 0.1027148 0.4073514 2.1590220
fit$braratio
#> [1] 0.4073514

The branching ratio is recovered close to the 0.5 the data were simulated with, and the baseline close to 1. Four kernels are available: exponential (memoryless decay), weibull and gamma (a delayed peak), lomax (a heavy tail: influence that fades slowly). Compare them by information criterion:

sapply(c("exponential", "weibull", "lomax"), function(k) {
  chwkfit(times, 300, kernel = k)$aic
})
#> exponential     weibull       lomax 
#>    352.1139    353.6296    354.1802

A sinusoidal baseline lets the background rate follow a cycle (a weekly or seasonal pattern) instead of staying constant:

chwkfit(times, 300, kernel = "exponential", base = "sinusoidal")$aic
#> [1] 352.3058

Checking the fit: time-rescaling residuals

If the fitted intensity is right, the compensator \(\Lambda(t_i) = \int_0^{t_i}\lambda(s)\,ds\) turns the event times into a unit-rate Poisson process (the time-rescaling theorem; Brown et al. 2002). chwkres() returns the rescaled inter-event quantities, which should then be uniform:

U <- chwkres(times, 300, "exponential", fit$theta)
stats::ks.test(U, "punif")$p.value
#> [1] 0.8168762
plot(stats::qunif(stats::ppoints(length(U))), sort(U), pch = 20, cex = .6,
     xlab = "uniform quantiles", ylab = "rescaled residuals",
     main = "Time-rescaling check")
abline(0, 1, col = 2)

A misspecified kernel shows as a departure from the diagonal; a p-value well below 0.05 says the model does not explain the clustering it was fitted to.

Events recorded to a resolution

Administrative data record dates, not instants. Many events on one day are ties, and a Hawkes likelihood with tied times is ill-defined (the kernel at lag zero). chwkjit() spreads each day’s events uniformly across that day, deterministically from a seed, so the fit is reproducible and no two events coincide:

days <- c(0, 0, 0, 1, 3, 3)
chwkjit(days)
#> [1] 0.1599104 0.2786011 0.7415649 1.3441907 3.0380302 3.8682281
anyDuplicated(chwkjit(days))
#> [1] 0

Fit on jittered times, and treat the day as the unit of the horizon.

Evaluating a likelihood directly

chwknll() returns the negative log-likelihood at given parameters, for profile plots, likelihood-ratio comparisons or a check against another implementation:

chwknll(times, 300, "exponential", fit$theta)
#> [1] 173.0569
chwknll(times, 300, "exponential", fit$theta * 1.5)
#> [1] 193.815

Boundaries and identifiability

A heavy-tailed kernel that tends to its exponential limit has a shape parameter that is not identified; the fit then stops at the parameter bound and says so (fit$atbound), instead of reporting an absurd estimate. Short series carry little information about the kernel’s shape: with a few hundred events, compare kernels by AIC but do not read much into the third decimal of a shape parameter.

Indigenous examples

Self-exciting models need event times (one row per event), which the published Indigenous health tables do not contain: they are counts and percentages. The bundled tables are used for gaps, trends and standardisation in vignette("applied-analyses"); a Hawkes model applies once event-level data are available under a community’s own data governance.

Stock and flow: population, admissions and length of stay

A custodial or clinical population can be described two ways: the stock (how many are held on an average day) and the flow (how many pass through in a period). The two are linked by the length of stay, and a published table that reports one without the others is easy to misread. These functions implement the standard measures (Lakner’s formulation, as used in the Canadian corrections statistics) with exact intervals.

Average daily population

The stock. Detention days in a period divided by the days in the period:

# Lakner's worked example: 3,000 inmates served 13,500 detention days in a year
adp(13500)
#> [1] 36.9863

# per-person days over a 30-day period give the same total
adp(c(10, 20, 30), t = 30)
#> [1] 2

adpcnts() estimates person-days from periodic headcounts (a monthly census) when daily data are not published.

Average length of stay

Detention days divided by the number of people who completed a stay:

# Lakner: 2,700 inmates admitted and released served 12,150 detention days
alos(12150, 2700)
#> [1] 4.5

Admissions implied by stock and stay

Little’s law ties the three together: in a stationary system, stock = flow × stay, so admissions = ADP × days / ALOS. Lakner (p. 20) runs it the other way: an average daily population of 25 and 1,750 admissions in a year imply a stay of 5.2 days, and back again:

alosimplied <- 25 * 365 / 1750
round(alosimplied, 1)
#> [1] 5.2
admits(25, alosimplied)
#> [1] 1750

Stock and flow together

stkflow() takes detention days and people per period, with an exposure (the population at risk) to turn counts into rates, and reports how the stock, the flow and the stay moved between periods. The pattern it is built to surface: fewer people, held longer, so the flow falls while the stock rises.

stkflow(
  days = c(115674, 126121), people = c(12647, 9608),
  prd = c("2023", "2025"),
  expo = c(15495050, 16256538)
)
#> ── Stock and flow over 2 periods ───────────────────────────────── 
#>  period people   days      alos      adp flowrate  stkrate
#>    2023  12647 115674  9.146359 316.9151 81.61961 2.045267
#>    2025   9608 126121 13.126665 345.5370 59.10237 2.125526
#> 
#>   2023 to 2025: people -24.0%, stay +43.5%, days +9.0%
#>   flow rate -27.6%, stock rate +3.9%  <- opposite signs: quote both
#> ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A table that reported only admissions would show a decline; one that reported only the daily count would show growth. Both are true, and the length of stay is the reason.

The distribution of stays

The mean stay is pulled far above the median by a few long stays, so the summary that matters is the whole distribution:

stays <- c(rep(1, 40), rep(3, 30), rep(10, 20), 60, 90, 120)
staysmry(stays)
#>    n totaldays     mean       sd median iqr max       se    lower    upper
#> 1 93       600 6.451613 16.33183      3   2 120 1.693532 3.088113 9.815113
staysmry(stays)[, c("mean", "median", "max")]
#>       mean median max
#> 1 6.451613      3 120

staysmry() gives the mean with its interval, the quantiles and the maximum, so a report can say “half of stays were three days or less, and the longest was 120” instead of “the average stay was 9.6 days”, which describes almost no one.

Periods and fiscal years

Published series use fiscal years and uneven periods:

perdays("2024-04-01", "2025-03-31")
#> [1] 365
fylbl(2024)
#> [1] "2023/24"

From a published table

  1. adp() for the stock from detention days, or adpcnts() from headcounts.
  2. alos() for the stay; admits() to check the implied flow against the published one.
  3. stkflow() across periods, with the population as exposure.
  4. staysmry() whenever per-person stays are available, and report the median.
